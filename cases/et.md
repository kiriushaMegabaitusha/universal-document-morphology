# Case: Ethiopia — Ge'ez chancery and the non-colonised state

The most interesting African case for a project about documents "away from the
empire": Ethiopia is the one African state that was **never colonised**, with
its own script (Ge'ez / Fidäl) and its own imperial chancery.

- **The record.** The National Archives and Library of Ethiopia, in Addis
  Ababa, was inaugurated in **1944** by Emperor Haile Selassie; the archives
  proper was established in **1979** [S-ET-NALE, C56].
- **The collection** includes ancient and historical manuscripts written from
  as early as the 14th–15th centuries, and **letters written by kings and
  empresses** — i.e. the surviving imperial correspondence [S-ET-NALE, C56].
- **Preservation is monastic.** Before and alongside the state archive,
  Ethiopian manuscript production and preservation happened in the nation's
  **monasteries and churches**; the institutional archive is a modern overlay
  on a much older ecclesiastical custody [S-ET-NALE].

## Why it matters

Ethiopia contributes three things no other case in the atlas does:

1. **A non-Latin, indigenous script** (Ge'ez/Fidäl) carrying the whole
   document — proof the slot grammar is independent of the alphabet, as the
   Islamicate and Chinese cases already hinted.
2. **A non-colonial state chancery**: the imperial letters are not the product
   of a colonial administration importing a European form, nor of the Ottoman
   or British Empire. The documentary order is Ethiopia's own.
3. **Custody by the church, not the state**, for most of its history — the
   archive as a religious, not a bureaucratic, institution. The 1979 state
   archive is a recent layer.

## The point for the atlas

Ethiopia is the strongest single counter-example to the suspicion that
"universal" document structure is really just "European structure". A
sovereign state with its own script, its own church, and no colonial
metropole still produced documents whose functional slots (origin, date,
body, authentication, custody) the atlas's model recognises. The alternative —
that this is coincidence, or that the surviving letters are atypical — has not
been excluded and is worth a dedicated study.
