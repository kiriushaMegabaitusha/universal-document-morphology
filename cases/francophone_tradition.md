# Case: the Francophone administrative tradition

A shared model used across France, the Benelux, Quebec, Switzerland and the
**Francophone African states** — Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, Burkina Faso, Mali,
Cameroon and others. Its distinctive contribution to the atlas is the
**formula**: the Francophone tradition prescribes not just what slots a
document has but the exact *phrases* that go in them.

Grounded here on the Luxembourg government's *Guide de rédaction* [S-LU-GUIDE]
and a French *Guide pratique de la rédaction administrative* [S-FR-AMIENS],
with the tradition's medieval root in the French royal chancery [S-FR-SORBONNE].

## 1. Two structured genres

- **La lettre administrative** rests on "une structure prédéfinie" whose parts
  are, in order: **en-tête** (letterhead), **destinataire**, **objet**,
  **formule d'appel**, **corps**, **formule de politesse**, **signature**
  [S-LU-GUIDE, C67]. The *objet* is written "Objet :", "Concerne" or "Conc."
  [S-LU-GUIDE].
- **La note administrative** and **la note de service** — the internal genres
  — are treated in the same guide [S-LU-GUIDE].

## 2. The formula system — the distinctive feature

The guide devotes whole chapters to *formules et locutions*: **selon le type
de lettre** (3.1) and **selon les différentes parties de la lettre** (3.2)
[S-LU-GUIDE, C68]. Two rules stand out:

- The **formule d'appel** (salutation) and the **formule de politesse**
  (complimentary close) **must match** and must be built from the recipient's
  *function*, never their surname [S-LU-GUIDE, C68].
- The canonical administrative formulas are fixed: *« J'ai l'honneur de vous
  faire connaître que… »*, *« J'ai le regret de vous informer que… »*
  [S-FR-AMIENS, C69].

A French guide also fixes the header furniture: **Objet** in bold halfway down
the page, **P. J.** (pièces jointes), and legal **références** (décrets,
articles de loi) [S-FR-AMIENS, C69].

## 3. The medieval root: the royal chancery

The Francophone administrative document descends from the **French royal
chancery**, whose diplomatics is fully documented [S-FR-SORBONNE, C72]: the
chancery produced a *typology of acts* — the **charte**, the **lettre
patente** (sealed with yellow wax on a double parchment queue), the
**mandement** (sealed on a single queue, or *clos*), and the **lettres closes**
— each distinguished by its seal and its authentication marks, including the
**royal souscription with monogram** and a *récognition*. Acts were entered in
registers (the *Trésor des chartes*, series JJ). The modern *bureau d'ordre*
(the registry that stamps, numbers and files every incoming and outgoing
paper) is the descendant of this chancery registration.

## 4. Document types

Beyond the letter and the note: the **circulaire**, the **arrêté**, the
**décret**, the **bordereau d'envoi** (a dispatch slip accompanying documents)
and the **soit-transmis** (a transmission endorsement) — the last two being
genres the Anglophone world handles with a sticky note and a CC line.

## The point for the atlas

The Francophone tradition is the atlas's clearest case of a document system
where the **formula, not the slot, is the standardised object**. Where the
Anglophone and Germanic standards fix layout and the Sinosphere fixes an
element grid, the Francophone tradition fixes *language*: a bank of permitted
phrases, indexed by letter type and by rank of addressee, with a hard matching
rule between salutation and close. That is what makes it recognisably
"administrative French" across four continents — and it is the direct
descendant of the medieval chancery's formulary.
