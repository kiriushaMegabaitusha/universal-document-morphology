# Case: Japan — seals (hanko) and the standardised page

## Authentication by seal, not signature

In the Sinosphere, seals are pressed on documents to establish authenticity
"similarly to signatures in the West". Japan calls them *inkan* (印鑑) or
*hanko* (判子); emperors, shōguns and officials used them to authenticate
documents, and they remain in daily official and business use
[S-EESEAL, C38]. Where the West developed the hand signature and the wax
seal, East Asia developed the **registered seal** as the primary
authentication device — an S12 realised entirely differently: it is
impersonal (the office, not the hand), it is registered as a legal materiel
(there is a public registration of your seal), and it is harder to forge than
a signature [S-EESEAL, C39].

Japan is today migrating this imprecise, ink-based authentication to
electronic signatures — the *hanko* reform — the same S12 slot re-encoded
digitally [S-EESEAL, C40].

## The standardised page

- **JIS Z 8301** (Rules for the layout and drafting of Japanese Industrial
  Standards) fixes the *structure of a standard document itself* — a
  meta-document standard, the Japanese analogue of the ISO/IEC Directives and
  of ДСТУ's form rules [S-JIS, C41].

## The point for the atlas

Japan isolates the **authentication slot** as the most variable of the
fourteen. The West: hand signature (+ wax seal, historically). East Asia:
registered seal (*hanko*), impersonal and officiary. The Ottoman Empire: the
sovereign monogram (tughra). Islamic courts: witnessed scribal attestation.
Estonia: cryptographic signature. Five realisations of one slot across five
cultures — the clearest demonstration that **which slot varies most is
authentication, not layout**. See `../diversity.md` §3.
