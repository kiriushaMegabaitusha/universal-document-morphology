# Case: the Inka khipu — a record system without a document

The boundary case. Kept deliberately, because a comparative morphology must
state where its model stops.

- The **khipu** (quipu) is a record-keeping device made of knotted cords, used
  across the Andes (c. 1100–1532 and beyond) [S-QUIPU, C44].
- The Inka used a **decimal positional system** of knots to store numbers;
  khipu could run from a few to several thousand cords [S-QUIPU, C45].
- They were used for **census records**, calendrical information and military
  organisation — the administrative backbone of the Inka state — and recorded
  "both statistical and narrative information" [S-QUIPU, C46].
- Cords are ordered "from most important to the least important category,
  according to colour, number, [and further features]" [S-QUIPU, C47].
- The system was adapted to colonial administration after the conquest, and
  unrelated knotted-string systems exist elsewhere (e.g. Chinese knotting)
  [S-QUIPU].

## Why it breaks the model

The 14-slot morphology presupposes a **document**: a bounded page-like object
with a linear sequence of slots, made to be read by a person in an order. The
khipu has none of that:

- there is no page, no linear reading order, no addressee, no signature;
- its "morphology" is an **encoding** morphology (cord hierarchy, knot type,
  colour, fibre) — closer to a database schema or a binary format than to a
  charter;
- it is *simultaneously* the record and the storage medium; there is no
  document/archive distinction to make.

So the khipu is a **record system without documents**. It is the proof that
the atlas's universality claim is scoped: the universal grammar holds for the
*written-official-document* genre, across cultures and centuries — not for
record-keeping as such.

## Slot profile

Not applicable. Attempting to force the 14 slots onto a khipu is itself the
finding: the model's domain of validity ends at the written document.
The only partially applicable slots are S2 (identifier — the khipu's own
provenance/registry) and the container notion (a khipu is a set of cords, like
a file), but neither maps cleanly.
