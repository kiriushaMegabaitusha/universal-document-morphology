# Case: Sweden — the transparency regime

The other kind of "document law": a regime that governs not the document's
format but its **accessibility**.

- **Principle:** the *principle of public access to official records*
  (offentlighetsprincipen) has existed in Sweden since **1766** and is
  enshrined in one of the country's fundamental laws [S-SE-ACCESS, C79]. It
  means everyone has the right to take part in [official documents].
- **Instrument:** the **Freedom of the Press Act**
  (*Tryckfrihetsförordningen*), whose first version dates to 1766, is one of
  the **four Fundamental Laws of the Realm** and thus part of the Swedish
  constitution; it regulates [the access principle among other things]
  [S-SE-FPA, C80]. The 1766 ordinance is widely described as the world's first
  legislation guaranteeing free communication [S-SE-FPA].

## The point for the atlas

Sweden supplies the atlas's **access dimension** with its historic origin:
where the other systems fix *what a document contains*, Sweden's constitutional
tradition fixes *who may read it*, and did so three centuries before the
Anglophone freedom-of-information acts. The document regime here is a
**rights regime**, not a formatting regime.

It pairs with Kenya (`ke.md`), which legislates public access to archives
statutorily — but Sweden is the ancestor, and it embeds access in the
constitution rather than in an archives act. Together they show that the
"access" dimension is not a document slot at all: it is a property of the
legal order that surrounds the document.

## Slot profile

No page slots (it is a regime). Governs the **access** dimension: who may read
the record — constitutionally, since 1766. Pairs with the trace dimension
(S14) and the persistence/appraisal dimension (see `ng.md`).

## Note on genre coverage

Sweden is included for the *access* tradition specifically. A fuller Nordic
entry would add the modern Swedish/ Norwegian/Finnish administrative writing
conventions, but the constitutional access tradition is the distinctive
contribution and is what the atlas was missing.
