# Case: Haudenosaunee wampum — a second record system without documents

The second boundary case, pairing with the Inka khipu: a non-alphabetic,
non-documentary record tradition, this one from North America.

- **Wampum** are beads made from white and purple shell (quahog and whelk),
  woven into belts [S-WAMPUM, C81].
- For the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), wampum served as **record-keeping and
  memory aids** — recording important treaties and historical events — and as
  **a person's credentials or a certificate of authority** [S-WAMPUM, C81].
- Named belts are themselves records: the **Two Row Wampum** (Guswenta /
  Kaswentha) and the **Hiawatha Belt** [S-WAMPUM, C81].
- The **Two Row Wampum Treaty** is a mutual agreement made in **1613** between
  the Five Nations and the Dutch in what is now upstate New York; its
  authenticity is carried by the *belt* and the oral recitation, while
  historians dispute the later **written** versions [S-WAMPUM-2ROW, C82].

## Why it belongs here

Like the khipu, the wampum belt is a **record system without documents**: no
page, no linear text, no signature, no addressee. Its "syntax" is the weave —
pattern, colour, and the relation of beads — read against a recited oral
tradition. The atlas's 14-slot morphology does not apply.

What it adds beyond the khipu is a sharper point about **authority**. The Two
Row Wampum's truth is carried by the belt and the recited words; the *written*
transcriptions are the disputed copies. This inverts the document-centric
assumption that writing is where authority lives: here the non-documentary
record is authoritative and the paper is the derivative. That is a finding
about the atlas's own premise, not just another column.

## Slot profile

Not applicable. The 14 slots presuppose a written document. The wampum adds a
second independent instance of the model's boundary (with `pe.md`), and
sharpens the authority question: which medium is binding when the record is
not written.

## Note

Kept deliberately, not as a curiosity: two independent non-documentary record
systems (Andean khipu, Haudenosaunee wampum) confirm that the atlas's
universality claim is scoped to the *written* official document, and that
record-keeping as such is broader than the document.
