# Case: West African Ajami and Timbuktu — documents in an African script

A documentary tradition that is neither European nor simply "Islamic": West
African manuscript culture, including administrative documents, written in
**Ajami** — African languages set in a modified Arabic script.

## Timbuktu

- The **Timbuktu manuscripts** were preserved for centuries not in an archive
  but in **private households** in Timbuktu (northern Mali); the collections
  cover art, medicine, philosophy, science and the Qur'an, against the
  background of the **Songhai Empire (1464–1591)** [S-ML-WIKI, C59].
- They are **predominantly in Arabic**, with a significant portion in
  **Ajami** — African languages written in Arabic script [S-ML-WIKI, C59].
- **4,203** of Timbuktu's manuscripts were **burned or destroyed** during the
  Mali War [S-ML-WIKI, C59] — the fragility of a record tradition with no
  state archive behind it.

## The wider West African corpus

Across West Africa there is a substantial body of manuscripts in Arabic and
Ajami that includes **correspondence, private letters and business records** —
i.e. administrative and commercial documents, not only religious texts
[S-ML-AJAMI, C60]. Ajami orthographies were devised for Dagbani, Fulfulde,
Hausa, Songhay, Tamasheq, Wolof, Yoruba, Zarma and others; important
collections run from the pre-colonial through the colonial period (IFAN in
Dakar; Hausa and Fulfulde Ajami in Nigeria) [S-ML-AJAMI, C60].

## The point for the atlas

This case kills the last possible objection to the universality claim: that
"universal document structure" might secretly mean "Latin-script European
structure". Here are **correspondence and business records in African
languages, in an African-modified script**, following the documentary
conventions of the Islamicate chancery rather than any European one — with the
same functional slots (origin, date, body, attestation, custody).

It also contributes a **custody model** the atlas had not seen: the archive as
the **family library**, decentralised, un-institutionalised, and correspondingly
vulnerable. The document persists because a household chose to keep it — not
because a state legislated it (contrast Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya).
