# Case: South Africa — NARSSA and the accountability record

A records-management regime rather than a page-layout standard: the African
case, and a state built after 1994 whose document discipline is
accountability-driven.

- **Body:** the National Archives and Records Service of South Africa (NARSSA)
  sets records-management standards for governmental bodies, in collaboration
  with the South African Bureau of Standards [S-SA-RMS, C48].
- **Mandate:** governmental bodies must "create and maintain authentic,
  reliable and usable records" to support continuing service delivery and
  accountability [S-SA-RMS, C49].
- **Codes of practice** are issued for managing electronic records, requiring
  infrastructure and process for digital records [S-SA-RMS].

## Why it belongs here

South Africa sits in the atlas's **Layer 2** (records-management standards),
but it shows that tradition doing work the European layout standards do not:
in a state emerging from authoritarian rule, the record is first of all an
*accountability instrument* — the evidence base for service delivery and for
holding officials answerable. The functional requirements (authentic,
reliable, usable) are the same words ISO 15489 uses; the political content is
different.

It is also the strongest non-Western example of a **statutory** recordkeeping
regime in the sample — records management as a legal duty, not an office
convention — and it is explicitly oriented toward *electronic* records from
the start, unlike the page-format standards.

## Slot profile

This is a Layer-2 regime: it does not fix page slots, so the 14-slot matrix
does not apply directly. It governs the **lifecycle** of whatever documents a
body creates, with a heavy emphasis on S14 (trace/audit) and authenticity
(S12) as continuants.

## Note

The atlas would benefit from a similar case from an East African or West
African jurisdiction (e.g. Kenya's or Nigeria's national archives acts) to
test whether the accountability framing generalises across the continent
rather than being specific to the post-apartheid transition.
